# Megawatt Valley — Visual Quality, World Character & Interaction Standard

**Status:** Product/design direction  
**Date:** 2026-10-07  
**Owner:** Rapha / ChatGPT product review  
**Purpose:** Translate the supplied Megawatt Valley inspiration images and videos into a durable quality bar for future implementation.

> This document describes the target feeling and quality standard. It is not an instruction to copy any reference literally. Megawatt Valley should remain visually original.

## 1. Core direction

The current true-3D direction is a strong foundation, but attractive 3D assets alone are only the baseline.

The target is a renewable-energy management world that feels **alive, authored, playful, readable and full of small discoveries**. The player should enjoy looking at the site even when they are not issuing commands.

The quality bar is closer to the care found in polished management/tycoon games: the world, characters, UI, animation, props, sound and feedback should all reinforce one coherent personality.

A useful rule:

**Macro gives the player a beautiful world.  
Meso makes it believable.  
Micro gives it soul.  
UI makes it pleasurable to manage.**

Do not optimise only for screenshot beauty. The world must communicate activity and respond visibly to play.

---

## 2. What the references establish

### 2.1 Macro — the valley must feel authored

The reference material uses strong composition rather than a flat field with objects placed on it.

Target qualities:
- readable terrain elevation and landforms;
- water, cliffs, vegetation and roads used to frame the playable site;
- infrastructure arranged as a believable operating facility;
- distinct zones with different silhouettes and identities;
- clear foreground/midground/background depth;
- roads and paths that visually connect the site;
- deliberate landscaping rather than uniform procedural scatter;
- open breathing room around important gameplay objects;
- strong visual landmarks so the player learns the site spatially.

Renewable infrastructure remains the hero. Scenery should support it rather than overwhelm it.

### 2.2 Meso — facilities must look used

A workshop should not merely be a workshop model. It should look like people work there.

Examples of useful detail:
- open workshop/service bays;
- parked or moving service vehicles;
- tool carts, crates, pallets, cones and maintenance equipment;
- fenced electrical areas;
- transformers, cabinets, cables and utility equipment;
- benches, bins, lamps, signs and barriers;
- landscaped office/amenity areas;
- small parking areas and marked access;
- paths between buildings;
- loading/service areas;
- equipment staged near active work;
- believable boundaries and gates;
- small interior glimpses where practical;
- site-specific clutter that changes by facility purpose.

Props should tell a story or improve readability. Avoid random clutter for clutter's sake.

### 2.3 Micro — this is where character appears

The references contain many small details that are individually inexpensive but collectively transform the scene.

Desired examples:
- workers walking with purpose;
- workers carrying tools/equipment;
- technicians visibly inspecting or repairing equipment;
- cleaning crews actually cleaning panels;
- people gathering briefly at facilities;
- staff entering/exiting workshops;
- vehicles arriving, parking and departing;
- forklifts or small utility vehicles doing believable work;
- birds/wildlife or local environmental life where appropriate;
- subtle humour;
- playful signs or speech/thought moments;
- small idle animations;
- tools/boxes appearing around an active maintenance job;
- dust, sparks, water, wipe/cleaning effects or other restrained task feedback;
- doors/gates opening when used;
- lights or indicators changing with equipment state.

The goal is not constant chaos. It is **evidence of life**.

A player who zooms in should regularly notice something charming, useful or amusing.

---

## 3. Workers are characters, not moving status icons

Staff are one of the most important opportunities to give Megawatt Valley personality.

Every worker should have:
- a recognisable visual identity/silhouette;
- a name and role;
- visible current activity;
- understandable movement and destination;
- task-specific animation where feasible;
- a sense that they belong to the physical site.

### Worker selection quality bar

Selecting a technician should feel like meeting a person, not opening a database record.

The worker inspector should aim for:
- a large, attractive character portrait or 3D presentation;
- name and role prominently shown;
- specialisation/skills;
- current task and destination;
- energy/wellbeing/status where relevant;
- assignment/site/duty;
- training/progression;
- concise performance or useful traits;
- contextual actions;
- clear iconography;
- strong visual hierarchy;
- subtle animation/transition on opening;
- personality without sacrificing management readability.

Do not permanently occupy large screen space with worker information. Detail appears contextually when the player asks for it.

Reference principle: study how polished management games make individual staff feel worth clicking, particularly Two Point Hospital/Campus and similar character-driven tycoon games. Learn the interaction principles; do not imitate their artwork or exact UI.

---

## 4. UI is part of the game's art, not an overlay

The interface must eventually reach the same quality level as the 3D environment.

Functional HTML-style panels are not the target.

Every major interaction should be deliberately designed:
- worker inspector;
- equipment/building inspector;
- build catalogue;
- objectives;
- research/capabilities;
- events;
- contracts;
- company management;
- notifications;
- tooltips;
- work orders;
- star/progression moments.

### UI qualities

Aim for:
- friendly but professional renewable-energy identity;
- clean hierarchy;
- strong typography;
- distinctive icons;
- tactile buttons and states;
- rounded/soft forms where appropriate;
- contextual panels instead of permanent dashboards;
- restrained use of colour for meaning;
- portraits/illustrations/3D previews where they add personality;
- useful hover states and tooltips;
- polished opening/closing transitions;
- clear selected/disabled/available states;
- satisfying confirmation feedback;
- minimal unnecessary text;
- no spreadsheet/admin-console feeling.

The valley remains the primary workspace. UI should reveal depth on demand rather than cover the world.

---

## 5. Interaction should feel tactile

A polished tycoon game communicates every meaningful action through several channels.

Where appropriate, actions should combine:
- visual state change;
- small animation;
- sound;
- concise text/icon feedback;
- world-space feedback;
- UI response.

Examples:
- placing equipment gives a convincing placement/build response;
- selecting equipment visibly identifies it in-world and opens relevant detail;
- dispatching a technician makes the worker's response and route understandable;
- completing a repair produces visible completion feedback;
- unlocking a capability feels like an achievement rather than a silent boolean change;
- earning a star should be a memorable progression beat;
- a fault should be discoverable in the world, not only in a notification list.

Avoid excessive particles, bouncing UI or constant popups. Feedback should be satisfying, readable and purposeful.

---

## 6. Environmental storytelling and humour

Megawatt Valley should have warmth.

The references demonstrate that tiny humorous or human touches can stop an infrastructure simulator feeling sterile.

Possible vocabulary:
- playful safety/site signs;
- worker thoughts or occasional speech;
- local wildlife doing harmless unexpected things;
- small staff interactions;
- visual jokes in props;
- changing noticeboards;
- branded site vehicles;
- employee names/personalities;
- celebratory behaviour after milestones;
- contextual details tied to events/weather.

Humour should be occasional and observational, not meme-heavy or disruptive.

The renewable-energy operation still needs to feel credible.

---

## 7. Animation and motion budget

The scene should never look frozen.

Use motion at different scales:
- slow environmental motion: trees/grass/water/clouds;
- operational motion: workers, vehicles, gates, tools;
- equipment motion where physically appropriate;
- event/weather motion;
- subtle UI motion;
- occasional characterful ambient moments.

Not everything should move simultaneously. The goal is a calm living site with pockets of activity.

Prefer many small believable motions over a few oversized effects.

---

## 8. Visual density: rich, not cluttered

The references are detailed, but detail is organised.

Use a hierarchy:
1. critical gameplay equipment;
2. staff and active work;
3. roads/paths/service areas;
4. supporting props;
5. decorative environment.

At normal gameplay zoom the player must immediately distinguish:
- buildable/owned infrastructure;
- faults and work states;
- staff;
- access/routes;
- selected object;
- objective-relevant items.

At close zoom the player should discover extra detail.

This creates **zoom reward**: readability from afar, personality up close.

---

## 9. Renewable-energy specificity

Do not allow the visual language to become a generic city builder with solar panels added.

The world should accumulate authentic renewable-site vocabulary:
- panel rows and realistic service spacing;
- inverters/combiner/electrical cabinets;
- transformers/substations;
- inspection and maintenance equipment;
- vegetation management;
- panel cleaning;
- weather exposure and soiling;
- service roads;
- operations/workshop areas;
- PPE and site safety;
- monitoring/communications equipment;
- future wind/BESS infrastructure when those systems arrive.

Authenticity and charm should coexist.

---

## 10. Audio is part of the eventual quality bar

Even though the supplied references are primarily visual, final polish should include a layered soundscape:
- environmental ambience;
- distant equipment/site sounds;
- footsteps/tools where useful;
- vehicle movement;
- restrained UI sounds;
- distinct positive feedback for completion/unlocks;
- weather/event audio;
- subtle worker vocalisations only if they suit the final tone.

Avoid noisy arcade feedback. Sound should reinforce place and tactility.

---

## 11. Quality test for any new feature

Before calling a player-facing feature polished, ask:

1. **Is it readable?** Can the player understand what is happening?
2. **Is it visible in the world?** Does simulation state have a physical expression where sensible?
3. **Is it tactile?** Does interaction provide satisfying feedback?
4. **Does it have character?** Is there an authored detail that makes it feel like Megawatt Valley?
5. **Does it respect the world?** Does UI avoid unnecessarily obscuring the valley?
6. **Does it reward closer inspection?** Is there extra detail at close zoom?
7. **Is it coherent?** Does it match the game's visual and interaction language?
8. **Is it useful?** Detail must not damage management readability.
9. **Is it performant?** Richness cannot make the game unpleasant to run.
10. **Would this look/feel at home in a premium management game?**

A technically working feature can still fail this quality test.

---

## 12. Implementation priority

Do not attempt all of this in one giant polish branch.

Recommended order:

### A. Interaction foundation
Establish the visual/UI design system and excellent inspectors for workers and equipment.

### B. Core activity visibility
Make existing repair, cleaning, servicing, construction and staff movement visibly happen in the 3D world.

### C. Facility storytelling
Add purposeful props and activity around workshops, offices, electrical infrastructure and service areas.

### D. Ambient life
Add restrained environmental animation, vehicles, wildlife, staff idles and occasional character moments.

### E. Milestone feedback
Upgrade unlocks, stars, events and progression moments.

### F. Continuous polish
For each future feature, include its world presentation and interaction polish as part of “done” rather than postponing all personality until the end.

---

## 13. Guardrails

- Do not copy reference art, characters, logos, layouts or proprietary assets.
- Do not turn the game into a visual-effects showcase at the expense of simulation clarity.
- Do not add clutter merely to fill empty ground.
- Do not use UI to compensate for simulation states that should be understandable in-world.
- Do not make every worker constantly speak or emote.
- Do not sacrifice performance for invisible micro-detail.
- Do not introduce another renderer/engine merely to chase this target; the target is quality and character, not a specific rendering technology.
- Do not interpret “Two Point-inspired” as “make it look like Two Point.” Study why polished tycoon interactions feel good, then create Megawatt Valley's own identity.

---

## 14. Acceptance vision

A successful Megawatt Valley scene should work at three distances:

**Zoomed out:**  
A beautiful, immediately readable renewable-energy valley with strong composition and obvious operational state.

**Normal play:**  
A convincing working facility where staff, roads, equipment, buildings, weather and management systems visibly interact.

**Zoomed in:**  
A charming miniature world full of purposeful details, individual workers, equipment activity, small stories and occasional humour.

And when the player clicks something, the interface should feel as considered as the world itself.

**The long-term target is not simply “good 3D.” It is a renewable-energy tycoon world with enough craft, feedback and personality that managing it is pleasurable and watching it is entertaining.**

## 15. Approved UI/UX concept — v1

**Decision date:** 2026-10-07  
**Status:** APPROVED DIRECTION / SOURCE-OF-TRUTH REFERENCE

Rapha approved the first clean-sheet UI/UX concept mockup as the direction to lock in.

### Product decision
- Keep the current true-3D world direction. It is an acceptable foundation, but still the minimum visual baseline rather than the final world-quality target.
- **Reject the current in-game UI/UX as a design baseline.** It is functional scaffolding only.
- The new UI must be designed clean-sheet. Do not preserve current layouts, panel structure, styling, information architecture or interaction patterns merely because they already exist.
- The approved concept establishes the desired visual/interaction language: polished management-game presentation, readable information hierarchy, generous but efficient spacing, contextual depth, characterful staff presentation, strong previews, useful comparisons and an attractive research/progression view.

### Approved concept covers four anchor surfaces
1. **Main gameplay HUD** — world remains dominant; compact top-level KPIs, objectives, contextual world labels, minimap/tools and a clear build/category rail.
2. **Solar building selection & details** — category navigation, multiple panel choices, larger selected preview, price/output/efficiency/maintenance/lifespan/footprint and suitability/trade-off information plus restrained real-world flavour/facts. The player should immediately understand why one panel is preferable to another for a particular strategy.
3. **Technician profile** — staff list plus large character presentation, name/role/speciality/personality, progression, skills, current work, wellbeing, salary, traits and contextual tabs/actions. Workers are characters, not rows in an admin table.
4. **Research / technology tree** — technology families, clear tiers/branches/dependencies, current research, research-point economy, completed work, locked future nodes and an immediately readable sense of where each branch leads.

### Codex instruction
**Codex must read this document before undertaking substantial player-facing UI/UX work.** It should also inspect the approved concept image when present at:

`Docs/References/megawatt-valley-ui-ux-approved-concept-v1.jpg`

The image is a **directional source of truth, not a pixel-perfect implementation contract**. Preserve the principles and quality bar while adapting details to real game systems, resolution, accessibility and implementation constraints.

When an existing UI conflicts with this approved clean-sheet direction, prefer the approved direction unless a later product decision explicitly overrides it.

### Design rule going forward
For every major player-facing surface ask:
- What decision is the player trying to make?
- What information is required to make it confidently?
- What information can remain secondary/on-demand?
- What preview, comparison or visualisation would make the choice intuitive?
- What atmosphere/character can be added without harming readability?
- Can the player understand progression, consequence and next steps at a glance?

Do not make a screen dense merely because the simulation has many variables. Use progressive disclosure: essential information first, deeper detail when requested.
